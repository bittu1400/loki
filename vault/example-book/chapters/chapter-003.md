# Chapter 3 — A Counter, A Gap

Ovist did not sleep that night. He did not try. He set the spring-tide page on the desk and turned the lamp down until the ink looked the colour of a bruise held under seawater, and he sat, and he did not look away.

In the morning the second-shift clerk found him in the same chair and said, mildly, that he looked like something the tide had refused to take back. Ovist said he was working. The clerk said he would hold the counter until noon, and Ovist said please, and meant it in a way that surprised him.

The Almanac Office kept its ledgers in a press of oiled drawers against the north wall, three deep. Each drawer held a year. Each year held twelve almanac pages, one for each spring tide, plus the supplementary leaves for the storm-tide depositions and the two quarterly reconciliations. The almanac pages were ruled in faint blue, a hand's width apart, and every entry on every page was countersigned by the receiving clerk at the bottom margin. That was the second rule. The first rule was that you logged what the tide brought. The second rule was that someone else had to agree you had seen it.

Ovist had countersigned eleven years of pages. He knew his own hand. He had written two corrections last night on a page he had not yet opened for business. The corrections were in his hand. The corrections were not his.

He pulled the year-drawer for the present annum first and worked backward, drawer by drawer, leaf by leaf, setting each almanac page on the desk and checking the countersignature column. It was a slow job. The lamp burned through its oil by the second drawer and he had to send a runner for more, and the runner came back with the lamp-keeper's boy who wanted to know if Ovist was unwell, and Ovist said no, thank you, and the boy stayed anyway in case he lied.

By the third drawer he had found the first gap. By the fourth he had stopped counting gaps and started counting years. Nine years. Nine years of spring-tide almanac pages with a missing countersignature on a single entry — always a single entry, never two — always on the spring tide, always near the margin, always a hole that did not look like a hole until you laid the pages against each other and let the missing ink show as a stripe of paler paper.

He laid them out on the floor. The runner helped, and then the lamp-keeper's boy helped, and by midmorning the pressroom floor was a pavement of spring-tide pages, each one missing a single signed line near the bottom. The missing lines, taken together, made a shape if you squinted. He did not squint. He was not that sort of man. He let the shape be a shape without giving it a name.

The boy said, "It's a person."

Ovist said, "It is a series of countersignature omissions consistent with a single absent clerk during a specific tidal window."

The boy said, "It's a person standing up."

Ovist did not answer. He was looking at the gaps and thinking about the spring-tide page on his desk and the two corrections in his hand that he had not made. Two corrections meant two entries. One gap meant one omission. He had been wrong about the arithmetic, or someone had been wrong about the arithmetic, or the arithmetic was the kind that changed depending on who was keeping it.

He went back to the desk and pulled the ledger for the year before the first gap. The pages were intact. He pulled the ledger for the year after the last gap. The pages were intact. He went through the intervening nine years a second time, and this time he noted the date of each missing countersignature beside the gap on a slip of his own, and when he had finished he set the slips in a row and counted the days between them.

The days were uneven. The days were very nearly even. The days were the interval, he thought, of someone returning to the harbour city of Vhal Mirek once in a generation, or twice, or as often as a generation permitted, which was not the same thing. He had read the hydrographer's tables. He knew what a generation was in tides. He knew what it was not.

The boy said, "Are you going to the Superintendent?"

Ovist said, "I am going to think for a quarter of an hour and not be spoken to."

The boy sat down on the pressroom floor among the spring-tide pages and put his hands on his knees, and he was quiet, and Ovist was grateful, and Ovist did not say so because he had been given a quarter of an hour and he intended to use it.

In the quarter of an hour he thought the following: that a record was only as honest as whoever was allowed to amend it; that the Office amended its records by countersignature, not by erasure; that a missing countersignature was not an erasure but it was, in the small and clerical sense, a permission granted to a ghost; that the spring-tide page on his desk had two corrections in his own hand, which meant that someone had, at some point, been allowed to write in his hand, which was a different problem from a missing signature and a worse one, and that the worse problem was sitting in his own notebook in his own ink waiting for him to decide what it was.

He stood up. The boy stood up. The pages on the floor rustled against each other like a small tide, and Ovist looked at them, and then he looked at the boy, and then he said, "Tell the lamp-keeper I will want a copy of the divers' bench log for the falling ebb as of yesterday, signed."

The boy said, "The lamp-keeper doesn't like to be told."

Ovist said, "The lamp-keeper will like it less if I come myself."

The boy went. Ovist knelt among the spring-tide pages and began to gather them into the order he had taken them down, and he worked slowly, and he did not count them, and when his hand paused over the oldest gap he let it pause, and he did not fill the pause with anything. The lamp on the desk burned. The page with his forged corrections lay under it, weighted by a river-stone he had not realised he had brought up from the slip.

He had eleven minutes left of his quarter of an hour. He used seven of them to file the pages back into the press, drawer by drawer, in the order they had been filed before, so that whoever came after him would find the office exactly as he had found it this morning, except for the page under the lamp, which he left where it was, because a thing under a lamp was, in the Almanac Office, the beginning of a record, and a record was what he was for.

In the remaining four minutes he wrote, on a clean slip, the single line he had been avoiding: *a person is missing from the spring-tide almanac and the almanac has been corrected to agree.* He set the slip on the desk beside the page. He did not sign it. He would not sign a thing he could not yet countersign, and he would not countersign a thing that was not yet a record, and he would not make it a record until he knew who had been permitted to write in his hand, and why, and how many times, and on which tides, and for which drowned.

The runner came back with the divers' bench log and set it on the desk and stepped away. Ovist opened it. The first entry was dated the previous day and named a diver he did not know, against every bench rule, at a depth he was not cleared for, and the lamp-keeper had signed it, which meant the lamp-keeper had chosen, for his own reasons, not to see.

Ovist sat down. He did not count the entries. He read the first one twice, and then he read it a third time, and then he set the log beside the slip and looked at the lamp and the page and the stone, and the morning tide came in below the office steps and touched the cut-water and went on, as tides did, and the record waited with him, as records did, for the next hand to come down and write in it.
