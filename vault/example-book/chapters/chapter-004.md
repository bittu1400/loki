The cellar had four lanterns and twelve feet of water-room above the flagstones when the tide was full. Now the ebb was down two fathoms and the walls smelled of herring scales and dry salt.

Sela Vosk sat on an upturned grease keg with a copper scraper in her palm. She was cleaning grease from three bits of green bottle-bottom that had come up from the reach before noon. She did not look up when the bulkhead latch dropped.

"Fourteen pieces," Brannec said. He laid the sack on the oak table. It made a flat, dull sound—wet hide against dry wood. "One was split at the edge. I left it on the ledge."

"The split ones have the best salt in them," Sela said. Her scraper made a dry *schip* sound against the green glass. *Schip. Schip.* "People cry into a mug or they cry into the wash-tub, but when they drown with their mouth open in the middle of a chop-sea, the water gets right into the grain of the bottle. You get sixty years of salt in a split neck. That's worth more."

"You get seventy pounds of lead down there too," Brannec said. He did not sit. His wool jersey was still damp at the shoulder where the fog had got in through the Rope Walk arches. "Office seals. Square-nailed."

The scraper stopped.

Sela Vosk had very small ears, flat against her head like a seal’s. She turned her head half an inch, the copper tool held still between her thumb and her first finger. "Lead seals?"

"Two of them. Bolted to the north face of the ebb ledge. Fresh lead. Not last year’s. Not ten years ago."

"The Office doesn't seal ledges. They seal casks. They seal the tallow-room. They seal the register chest when the clerk goes to his dinner."

"They sealed the rock," Brannec said. "With an eight-inch iron spike and a brand in the grease."

She set the glass down on the keg top. She did not reach for the sack. Her hands were gray with pumice-dust from rubbing the drift-marks off clear glass so the candlemakers would buy it without asking whose kitchen window it had dropped out of.

"Who was down there?" she asked.

"Nobody. The water was five fathoms. The silt was down to the floor-ribs. But somebody had an iron cold-chisel and four pound of lead and a tallow pot to keep the damp off the stone while they hammered."

"The Almanac Office doesn't use cold-chisels," she said. "They use pens. They use black ink that smells of gall and vinegar. If they’re down at the nine-fathom mark with a hammer, it's not to log a drowned smith."

"They logged someone out," Brannec said.

The room was very quiet. Above them, on the stone quay, an iron-shod cart wheel went past, the axle screaming for grease. Then the sound died away into the salt-fog.

Sela Vosk stood up. She was shorter than him by the breadth of a palm, but she had the wide, heavy shoulders of someone who spent twenty years hauling baskets of wet kelp up the Slip without a block and tackle. She walked to the corner where a cask of brackish water stood with a dipper hanging from the stave. She did not drink. She just touched the rim of the dipper with one finger.

"You want the triple rate for the green ones?" she asked.

"I want to know who was on the ledge before the ebb turned."

"You won't find him in the bench log. The lamp-keeper has three nephews and two of them are idiots who can't hold a measuring line straight, and the third one is the one who keeps the books. He writes what the clerk tells him to write. Or what the man with the silver buckles tells him to write."

"The clerk was Ovist Rhoam," Brannec said. "He doesn't have silver buckles. He wears shoes with iron nails that he hammers in himself when the heads wear down."

"Then it wasn't him," Sela said. She turned around. Her eyes were small and dark, like two dried sloes pressed into dough. "Ovist doesn't go below the second step. He gets the vertigo if he looks down the well-hole when the wind's from the east. I seen him turn green when a dead dog floated past the office slip."

"Someone's using his hand," Brannec said.

"Whose hand?"

"His."

Sela Vosk walked back to the grease keg, picked up her copper scraper, and made one long, slow pass down the belly of a brown gin bottle. The metal hissed against the glass.

"There's a window in the back of the North Gate guardroom," she said, not looking at him. "The one they bricked up after the plague year because the draft came through and put the tallow dips out. The mortar's old. It's lime and sand from the bay. If you take a flat iron—not a chisel, a flat iron about as long as your finger—you can work the bottom three bricks out without making a clatter. There's a bench behind the brickwork. A man can sit there with his knees up to his chin and watch the register chest through the grating."

Brannec waited.

"You want to know who's writing in the clerk's book?" she said. "You sit in the dark behind the brickwork for four hours after the night-tide turns. You don't take a lamp. You don't smoke your pipe. You just smell the mortar and wait for the man with the key."

"And if I do?"

"Then you bring me the iron seal you found on the ledge."

"The lead?"

"The lead," she said. "The whole piece. With the square nail through the middle of it. I want to see what's stamped on the back. The Office doesn't use the old crown stamp anymore. They changed it when the new mayor came in three winters back. If that lead has the old crown on it, it's not the Almanac Office down there. It's the salt-tax men. And if it's the salt-tax men, they aren't erasing drowned people because they're sad about the water."

"Why are they doing it?"

"Because you can't tax a corpse," she said, and her scraper hit a bubble in the glass with a little *tink* like a small bell under water. "And you can't collect wharf-dues from a man who went down six fathom deep with his horse and his timber-cart if the book says he never arrived at the gate."

Brannec looked at the sack on the oak table. The green glass inside was cold; he could feel the chill coming through the coarse hemp.

"The divers' bench log says I was at nine fathoms yesterday," he said.

Sela Vosk stopped scraping. She looked at him then, her eyes hard and still. "Were you?"

"I was at seven."

"Then the book lied about two fathoms of water," she said. "Which means it can lie about two yards of earth, or two names on a list, or two hundred head of cattle that never came out of the north hold. You want your silver?"

She reached into her apron pocket and pulled out four heavy shillings, old ones with the edges clipped thin by someone who had been dead twenty years. She laid them on the wood, one by one, until they made a little square.

"That's for the green ones," she said. "The lead seal is extra. You bring it to me before the morning tide hits the cut-water, and I'll tell you whose name was under the one they scraped off the register two years ago."

"Whose name?"

"My brother's," she said. She didn't sound angry. She sounded like she was reading the price of salt-fish off a board in the market. "He went down with the coal lighter when the cable parted in the reach. They logged him as a runaway. They said he took the boat and went south to the salt-pans. But his boots were in the locker under his bunk, and nobody walks twelve mile to the pans without their boots in the middle of January."

She picked up her scraper again.

"The North Gate window," she said. "Don't use an iron bar. Use a cold chisel. It makes less noise if you tap it with the heel of your hand instead of a hammer."

Brannec took the four shillings. They were cold against his palm. He dropped them into his pocket one at a time, listening to the small dull clink they made against his knife-handle.

"The lead's at the bottom of my box," he lied.

"Go get it then," she said, turning the green bottle over in her lap. "The tide's turning. If you wait till midnight, the cellar will be knee-deep and you'll have to wade for your boots."
