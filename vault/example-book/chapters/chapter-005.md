The water clock on the east wall dripped eleven past the hour of the goat, which meant Ovist had been standing at the tall cabinet for nine of those drops, and had not opened a single drawer.

The cabinet held the countersigning rolls. Twelve years of them, bound in oilcloth and shelved by the clerk whose hand had countersigned the original entry: a second signature required on any echo that might later be disputed, any drowning whose body had not come up, any driftglass whose voice had been too faint to log with confidence the first time around. There were three hundred and seven such echoes in the spring-tide ledger alone. Eleven years of his tenure, then one year of his predecessor, Ferain Hoss, who had kept the cabinet under lock and who had taught Ovist, in the first week of his clerkship, that the second hand on a countersigning was not a courtesy but a second pair of eyes on a man going down.

Ovist had written the corrections in his own hand and not known it. That was the part he could not get past. Not the corrections themselves — corrections happened, ink blots happened, a hand reached across the page and amended a number and the next clerk carried on. It was the *hand* that stayed in his chest like a swallowed fishbone. The slant of the downstroke, the way the t-crossing leaned left instead of right because he wrote faster than he thought, the small hook on the tail of the *y* in *drowned* — all his. He had held the spring-tide page up to the lamp at half past nine and read it like a letter from himself, and the letter had said: this is not what you saw.

He pulled the first roll.

*Ferain Hoss, Clerk of Record, Retired.* Bound in oilcloth the colour of weak tea, the corners rubbed soft. He set it on the reading stand and untied the green tape and opened the cover, and the smell of old ink came up at him like a hand over his mouth.

He worked the way he had been taught. Each countersigning was numbered in the margin by the originating clerk, then keyed to the roll by Hoss's own hand: *see entry*, *confirmed*, *amended*, *struck, see new entry*. Ovist had done this work ten thousand times. He could tell a clerk's mood from the pressure of the nib. Ferain Hoss, in life, had pressed hard. The downstrokes were furrows. The man had not so much written as carved.

The spring-tide corrections were not in Hoss's roll.

He checked twice. The numbers on the margins of the corrections did not appear anywhere in Hoss's countersigning. They had been written *past* him, after his year of tenure, by the hand that came after Hoss — the hand that came after Hoss on every roll, every entry, every amended line in the twelve years since.

He set Hoss's roll aside. He pulled the second.

*Edra Munn.* No. Wrong.

The third.

*Rovas Pell.* No.

The fourth.

The cabinet was not large. There were only nine rolls ahead of his own. He worked through them in order, because order was what he had, and because if he stopped now he would have to stand again at the cabinet and that would be another nine drops on the water clock he did not have.

By the time he reached the ninth roll, the lamp had burned down a full inch and his left hand had begun to cramp around the page-turner he used for the older entries, the brittle ones whose spines cracked if a man breathed on them wrong. The ninth roll was bound in oilcloth the colour of road mud. The label was written in a small, upright, rather finicky hand that Ovist knew as well as his own, because he had trained under it for a season and a half before being moved to the tide tables.

*Pell Avor, Acting Clerk of Record, 6th of Brass to 9th of Goat, Year of the Leaden Spoon.*

Acting. The word had never struck him before. There had been no one in the Almanac Office called Pell Avor since Ovist's first winter, and Avor had died that spring of a swelling in the throat that took him in three days, and Ovist had been the one to log the death in the auxiliary because the man had no living kin and no one else would sit with the body long enough to write it down. Ovist remembered the page. He had copied the cause of death himself from the physician's note, because Pell Avor's hand had become unreadable in the last week.

He opened the roll.

The corrections were there. Both of them. In his own hand. Keyed to Avor's countersigning number, which Ovist had to look up three times before he believed it, because Pell Avor had died six years before half the entries he was supposed to have countersigned had even been written.

Ovist closed the roll. He set it on the reading stand. He put both hands flat on the oilcloth and stood there, breathing, while the water clock dripped the hour of the goat toward the hour of the bell.

*Procedural,* he told himself. *There is a procedure for this.*

There was not. The procedure was for clerks who amended their own work and confessed it by morning, or for clerks who amended the work of the living and could be brought before the bench to answer for it. There was no procedure for a dead man's countersigning appearing in a living man's hand, on a page that had not been opened in a season. There was no drawer in the cabinet marked *when the dead begin to sign for us.*

He counted the entries. There were nine. Not corrections — of those there were two, and both of them his — but nine countersignings that pointed, by their margin numbers, to a clerk dead six years. He stood at the cabinet for a long time, and his lamp burned, and the water on the office roof sounded, to his ledger-ear, like his own name said nine times by nine different throats under water.

He untied the green tape on his own roll. He set it beside Avor's. He did not open it yet. He looked at them, the two of them, side by side on the reading stand in the small lamp-light — his oilcloth and the dead man's oilcloth — and he thought, with the part of his mind that still knew how to file a thing, that whatever was happening to the ledger of the drowned had been happening longer than he had been alive to see it, and that he had only just now been written into it.
